If a model wrote part of an article, the reader should be able to find that out without
hunting for it. Everything else is detail, but the detail is where this gets decided.

## Three things worth stating plainly

**Who wrote it.** Not "this newsroom uses AI tools", which is true of everyone and tells a
reader nothing. Which model, on which piece.

**What a person did about it.** "Reviewed by" is a meaningful claim and an empty one
depending on whether anybody checked the sources. If a human read it and signed off, say
who. If nobody did, say that instead.

**What it was based on.** A list of sources with links is worth more than a paragraph of
methodology. It is also checkable, which is the point.

## What not to do

Do not put a model's name in the byline position where a person's name goes. A byline is a
claim about responsibility, and a model cannot hold any.

Do not bury the disclosure in a site-wide policy page. A reader deciding whether to trust
one article is not going to go looking.

Do not use "AI-assisted" as a shield. It covers everything from a spellcheck to a first
draft, and a phrase that covers everything discloses nothing.

## The uncomfortable part

Some of this costs you. An honest attribution line on a piece a model drafted will lose
some readers who would otherwise have read it. That is not a bug in the disclosure; it is
the disclosure working. Readers are entitled to that preference and it is not the
newsroom's business to route around it.
